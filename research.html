Engineering Biological Computation through Bioconvergence

Research

Biological computation emerges from interactions among molecular signalling, cellular dynamics, network topology and environmental feedback. No single scale explains how living systems compute. Our research determines how information is represented, transmitted, transformed and stored across these scales, and how these mechanisms can be engineered within living neural systems.

We pursue this through bioconvergence: the functional integration of biological substrates, computational representations and engineered interfaces. Mechanistic models identify candidate principles. Biofabrication controls cellular organisation. Electrophysiology measures and perturbs network dynamics. AI and digital-twin frameworks connect experimental observations with prediction and control.

The engineering problem

Living neural systems exhibit rich information-processing dynamics, but their computational functions remain difficult to design, reproduce, predict and control. The unresolved problem is to determine how molecular signalling, cellular interactions, network topology, stimulation and interface dynamics jointly generate measurable computational functions.

The programme investigates how living systems represent, transmit, transform and store information; which molecular, cellular and network mechanisms generate computation; whether cellular composition, topology and stimulation can be engineered to produce specified functions; and whether bioelectronic interfaces and computational models can make living computation more predictable and reproducible.

Research architecture

Discover → Engineer → Couple → Predict and control

Discover

We investigate how calcium signalling, neuron–astrocyte interactions, neuronal activity and network topology regulate information transmission and computation. This work establishes mechanistic and quantitative descriptions of living systems across molecular, cellular and network scales.

  • Molecular and calcium communication
  • Neuron–astrocyte dynamics
  • Stochastic biological signalling
  • Network topology and inference
  • Demyelination, inflammation and information loss
  • Multiscale biological mechanisms

Engineer

We develop computational models and experimental strategies for controlling the structure and dynamics of living neural networks. Current work connects neuronal logic, memory, reservoir dynamics and topology-dependent computation with biofabricated cultures, organoids and MEA-based biological testing. Neuronal gates and sequential circuits have so far been demonstrated computationally.

  • Neuronal logic and sequential circuits
  • Memory and plasticity
  • Topology-dependent computation
  • Biologically plausible reservoir computing
  • Structured neural cultures
  • Biofabrication
  • Organoid and cellular computational substrates

Couple

We develop interfaces through which living activity can be measured, stimulated, represented and predicted. Bioelectronic interfaces, mechanistic models, AI and biological digital twins provide the components required to connect living dynamics with digital and electronic systems.

  • MEA electrophysiology
  • Bioelectronic and neural interfaces
  • Stimulation and measurement
  • Intra-body and multiphysical communication
  • AI-enabled biological analysis
  • Biological digital twins
  • Biohybrid systems

Research contributions

Barros's established contributions provide quantitative, mechanistic descriptions of biological communication. Communication-theoretic and control models of calcium signalling in tissues and astrocyte networks establish how signalling dynamics, connectivity and noise constrain information transmission. Complementary work quantifies how demyelination and inflammatory signalling degrade neuronal communication and information capacity, and develops network-topology inference methods for reconstructing functional connectivity from recorded activity.

A second line of established work addresses computation directly. Models of tripartite-synapse logic and sequential neuronal circuits demonstrate, computationally, how neuron–astrocyte interactions can implement Boolean and sequential operations; this modelling is complemented by collaborative MEA experiments showing how astrocytes desynchronise and reduce the predictability of cultured neuronal networks. A multiscale digital-twin framework connects these cellular and tissue-level models with organ-level physiology, mapping where in-silico pharmacology can complement experimental and clinical measurement.

UC² Laboratory and research environment

Michael Barros founded the Unconventional Communications and Computing Laboratory at the University of Essex in 2022. UC² brings together biocomputing, molecular communication, neural interfaces and biofabrication.

The laboratory combines computational modelling with cell culture, MEA electrophysiology and bioprinting. Its core team currently comprises three researchers, supported by technical and collaborative expertise across engineering and biological sciences.

Infrastructure

At Essex, he founded the UC² Laboratory and established cell-culture capability within a computer science and engineering department. He secured more than £200,000 of infrastructure, including an Axion MEA system, bioprinting, incubation, centrifugation, refrigeration and supporting culture equipment.

People and community

He has advised six PhD researchers, 38 MSc researchers, 31 undergraduate researchers and five visiting researchers. He co-leads the Alan Turing Institute Special Interest Group on Learning the Organisational Principles of Living Systems and contributes to research assessment through the EPSRC Peer Review College, EPSRC fellowship panels and the BBSRC Technology Missions Fund Engineering Biology panel.
